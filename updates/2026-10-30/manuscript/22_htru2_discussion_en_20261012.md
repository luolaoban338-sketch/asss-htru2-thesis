# Discussion

## Compression did not ensure preservation of predictive performance

The HTRU2 evaluation supports a bounded conclusion: the joint procedure can reduce the training matrix, but the resulting classifier does not consistently preserve the specified F1 tolerance. Retaining approximately 35% of matrix cells is a concrete reduction in the data passed to final model fitting. Its value depends on the prediction losses and the computation needed to select those cells. The observed tolerance failures and lower stability therefore qualify the practical benefit of compression rather than invalidate the measured reduction itself.

The comparison with sequential selection identifies a trade-off between matrix size and predictive performance. Joint selection retained slightly fewer cells while yielding a lower mean F1. However, the procedures selected different row and feature budgets. The comparison consequently evaluates their overall decisions; it does not identify which scoring module produced the difference. The subsequent fixed-budget ablation addresses those conditional effects at one common matrix size and finds no incremental selected-set or F1 change for the tested removals. It does not explain the earlier difference between adaptive joint and sequential decisions.

Inner feasibility also has a narrower meaning than a held-out performance guarantee. The selection rule uses an empirical inner F1 threshold to choose among candidates. Its success on that threshold does not establish noninferiority on outer data. Variation between training partitions, estimation error in contributions and changes in selected features could each contribute to the observed failures. The present experiments do not determine their relative contributions, so revising the tolerance after seeing these outcomes would change the research question rather than validate the original rule.

## Contribution regularization and selection stability are different targets

The stability analysis tests an outcome that is distinct from predictive utility. Nogueira, Sechidis and Brown [2] characterize feature-selection stability in terms of sensitivity to data sampling and algorithmic randomness. In this setting, joint selection was less stable than sequential selection under the complete-workflow resampling protocol. This result constrains the interpretation of the proposed stability penalty: penalizing estimated contribution variability did not ensure a more reproducible final feature set in this development evaluation.

Several explanations remain compatible with that observation. Small differences in contribution estimates may change a greedy path, and the inner search can change both the sampling rule and the feature budget. Variables with similar predictive utility might also substitute for one another. The experiments did not separately manipulate these mechanisms. In particular, truncating corrected variance at zero is an estimator operation, not evidence that a feature is intrinsically stable. The fixed-budget ablation now supplies that paired comparison: the same selected sets persisted with and without the penalties. Thus, perfect set stability at k=4 cannot be attributed to the contribution-variability penalty. The contrast with complete-workflow instability is consistent with a role for budget or sampling-rule variation, but the two designs do not identify a unique cause.

The exact-reference checks help distinguish numerical correctness from approximation adequacy. Agreement of independent exact calculations supports the implementation on the saved eight-variable games. Differences between estimated and exact paths nevertheless show that the fixed permutation budget can change selection. Increasing that budget reduced contribution error in the standalone diagnostic sweep, but those observations did not determine a sufficient budget for reliable path recovery. Approximation uncertainty remains part of the evaluated procedure and must not be silently removed from subsequent interpretation.

## Contamination sensitivity depends on the metric and the selection rule

The contamination experiments qualify any robustness claim in two ways. First, lower clean-to-contaminated loss can arise alongside lower absolute contaminated F1. Second, unchanged budgets can conceal substantial changes in selected rows and variables. These findings require performance, selected identities and retained matrix size to be interpreted together. An invariant random sample, for example, is a property of frozen row identities and seeds; it does not show that its fitted classifier resisted corrupted predictors.

The joint stress test held the clean decisions fixed and rebuilt the selected identities under perturbation. It therefore estimates the sensitivity of an existing decision, not the capacity of a new inner search to adapt to contamination. The comparable F1 values of the restricted random, ordinary and robust candidate rules provide no consistent advantage for the robust rule under this design. They also cannot isolate geometry because the inherited fold-specific decisions can differ between rules.

ROC-AUC and average precision add information about ranking, whereas F1 evaluates predictions at the frozen threshold. Their different numerical changes do not justify claiming that one metric is unaffected or that ranking quality guarantees useful thresholded predictions. Threshold calibration could be studied separately using training data, but adjusting it after inspecting the present outer outcomes would no longer assess the frozen procedure.

## Reproducibility and applicability have separate boundaries

The audit trail makes the reported decisions inspectable: saved splits, selections, predictions and checksums connect each summary to a completed run. Successful replay establishes reproducibility under the recorded implementation and environment. It does not establish generalization beyond HTRU2, nor does it correct the dependence between overlapping training folds, resamples or repeated contamination draws. These units support descriptive comparisons within the development design, not independent replications across datasets.

HTRU2 is a compact benchmark of pulsar candidates with eight numerical predictors [1]. The repository does not provide positional information or other astronomical details needed to verify source-level separation. The local descriptor-order discrepancy further limits physical interpretation of individual selected columns. Accordingly, this work evaluates a tabular classification procedure; it does not establish discovery performance on independent astronomical sources. The imposed numerical corruption is likewise a controlled stress rather than a validated model of instrument noise.

Matrix compression also leaves the cost of discovering the reduced matrix unresolved. The present computation includes repeated utility fitting and exact diagnostics, while shared candidate caches prevent separate end-to-end timing comparisons between decision rules. The evidence supports a reduction in final training inputs, with no demonstrated general speed advantage. A later cost study would need isolated executions and an explicit distinction between deployment-required selection work and optional diagnostic work.

## Fixed budget invariance narrows the unresolved questions

The ablation separates a final selection outcome from changes in the process that produced it. Under the tested coefficients and four-feature budget, module removals left the endpoint unchanged even when complete paths differed. Strong separation between the chosen and unchosen variables, or penalties too small to cross the relevant selection boundary, could each explain this pattern. The experiment did not manipulate these explanations independently, so the zero contrasts do not establish that the modules are generally irrelevant.

The random-four control adds a different comparison. Its lower average F1 and less consistent feature sets show that the common feature count alone does not account for the scoring-rule outcome. However, negative fold differences remain, and the shared selected set across all scoring variants prevents attribution to an individual module. Stable compression also remains distinct from useful prediction: an invariant subset can still lose F1 relative to using all variables.

The next discriminating question is whether module effects emerge when the selection boundary changes under a prospectively frozen design. A new dataset or independently held-out development split, with prespecified budgets and coefficients, could distinguish local insensitivity from a broader absence of incremental value. Repeated adaptive workflows with separately controlled data and algorithm seeds would address the source of instability. These are prospective research questions, not evidence supplied by the current experiment or authorization to retune its completed runs.

The present contribution is an auditable account of joint compression, its predictive losses and its distinct forms of selection variability on HTRU2. Reproducible execution supports that account, while external validation, isolated selection cost and the general value of the scoring modules remain unresolved.
